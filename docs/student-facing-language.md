# Student-facing language

This guide applies to every message a student sees in EconPlanner, including the intake form, plan comparison, course changes, warnings, and the downloadable report. It also applies to information shown to a student in a section labeled for an advisor.

## Write for a student planning college classes

- Use familiar words such as “classes you still need,” “course load,” “term,” “schedule,” “advisor,” and “graduate.” Keep official course names and course codes accurate.
- Use short sentences, active voice, and one main idea at a time. Tell the student what happened and what they can do next.
- Explain college terms when they matter. For example, call a prerequisite “a class you need to take first” the first time it affects a decision.
- Name the reason for a warning. “Needs advisor attention” must be followed by what needs review and why. When possible, give the student a clear question to take to an advisor.
- Show a reminder only when the student’s plan or records make it relevant. Name the affected scheduled class and its prerequisite; for seat checks, name the classes whose current seat availability is unconfirmed.
- Group prerequisite reminders when the same class unlocks several scheduled classes or several prerequisites lead to one scheduled class. Keep the course codes and terms so the student can see the order across the full plan.
- Use a calm heading that suits a multi-year plan, such as “Things to keep in mind,” rather than implying every reminder must be acted on immediately.
- Put the most useful choice or next step first. Use a short explanation beside it; put extended checks and source records behind a clearly named disclosure.
- Use action labels such as “Change,” “Apply change,” and “Undo last plan edit.” Avoid repeating a plan’s name in both its heading and its button.
- When a course cannot move, lead with one plain reason: for example, “No conflict-free section is available in the published Winter 2027 schedule,” “This term is at its course limit,” or “We can’t confirm this course in a future schedule yet.” Keep the detailed check and its sources available on request.

## Keep academic meaning exact

- Never turn missing or uncertain evidence into a definite claim. Say what the student’s answers show and what remains unknown.
- Do not imply that a planned class is completed, that an unpublished class will be offered, that a seat is open, or that an advisor has approved something.
- Keep eligibility, requirements, course limits, prerequisites, timing, and approval rules unchanged. Plain language changes how a rule is explained, not what the rule means.
- Keep policy identifiers, provenance, diagnostic codes, and exact source text in internal records or clearly labeled supporting detail. Do not make students read them to understand the decision.
- Do not repeat the same reason in multiple panels. If a status needs explanation, show a short reason near the status and one complete explanation in the next-steps area.

## Research cues

The wording standard combines professional plain-language and interface-copy guidance with vocabulary used by college planning pages. GOV.UK recommends clear, direct wording and scannable structure; Nielsen Norman Group recommends concise, specific interface copy; Material Design recommends labels that make button actions clear. College planning and advising pages commonly use phrases like “classes you still need,” “plan next semester,” and “course load.” Student forum discussions offer useful informal vocabulary cues, but are anecdotal and do not establish academic policy.
